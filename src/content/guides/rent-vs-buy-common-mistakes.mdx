Most rent versus buy comparisons are not wrong because of the arithmetic.
They are wrong because of the assumptions, and the assumptions are usually
made silently.

The <a href="/tools/rent-vs-buy/">rent versus buy calculator</a> on this site
makes each assumption an explicit input, so the effect of changing it is
visible rather than buried. This guide covers the ones that most often swing
the answer.

## Treating appreciation as a promise

The most consequential error, because it works in one direction. A comparison
that assumes the home rises at 3 percent a year and omits the possibility of
a fall produces an answer that is not true in the case that matters most.

The number is an assumption, and a reasonable one can be supported with data:
the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis publishes long-run house price indices
that are a reasonable starting point for calibration. But the spread between
good and bad decades is enormous, and the arithmetic of a five-year horizon
is entirely different from the arithmetic of thirty years.

The fix is to run the calculation more than once: with the appreciation at
zero, then with it positive, then with it negative. If the answer changes
completely, the answer was never really about rent versus buying. It was
about a house price forecast.

## Forgetting that owning has running costs

Rent is one number. Owning is a mortgage payment, plus property tax, plus
insurance, plus HOA, plus maintenance.

Maintenance is the one that gets left out, because it is the one that does
not arrive with a bill attached to it. Roofs, appliances, water heaters and
paint do not show up on any statement, and they do not appear in a
comparison that only lists mortgage and tax.

The right treatment is a percentage of the purchase price per year, entered
as an explicit number that can be argued about. A percentage of the price is
not a claim about a particular house; it is a rough proxy for the fact that
a building ages.

## Leaving the down payment out of the comparison

The down payment is cash that leaves the savings account. A complete
comparison asks what that cash would otherwise have earned, and compares that
against what putting it in the house produced.

Most simple comparisons do not do this, and the omission is large. A
twenty-percent down payment on a $450,000 house is $90,000 — money that is
gone from savings for the duration, and money whose alternative use is
part of the decision.

The rent versus buy calculator on this site does not model the return on the
down payment either. It reports the cash outlay and the equity built, which
is the part of the comparison the arithmetic can establish. The rest is a
judgement about returns.

## Using a headline rent and a headline price

Rents and prices are quoted in a way that flatters one side of the
comparison. A building with a nice apartment and a bad building five minutes
away can carry the same monthly rent; a house with a good kitchen and a
house with a bad roof can carry the same price. The comparison treats them as
interchangeable, and they are not.

## Comparing over a horizon that does not match the plan

A thirty-year horizon is a poor assumption for a person who expects to move
in four years, and a four-year horizon is a poor assumption for a person who
buys to stay. The horizon is the most powerful lever in the whole comparison
after the appreciation input, because the upfront cost of buying is
amortised over the years you stay.

Running the calculation at five years and at thirty years, with everything
else the same, shows this clearly.

## Comparing the monthly number instead of the total

The monthly comparison is the same error that appears in mortgage
comparisons. A rent of $2,400 against an ownership cost of $2,150 makes
renting look more expensive, and it may be, month by month. Over the
horizon, with the equity accounted for, the ordering can be different. The
crossover month exists precisely because the monthly answer and the total
answer disagree for a long time.

## Ignoring the exit

Both paths have one, and they are different. Renting has a deposit, a notice
period and the cost of moving. Owning has selling costs, a market that may
be softer than it was, and a mortgage that has to be paid off or assumed by
the next buyer.

The calculator reports selling costs at the end of the horizon, because they
are a real and often significant figure. It does not model the risk of
selling into a weaker market, which is a fact about the future rather than
about the arithmetic.

## A practical order of operations

If you are running the comparison for real, this order tends to expose the
assumptions that matter:

1. Get the actual price, the actual rent, the actual tax rate and the actual
   insurance quote for the specific properties.
2. Run the calculation with a flat house price, and record the crossover.
3. Run it again with a negative price change, and again with a positive one.
4. Run it at your likely horizon and at thirty years.
5. Look at how much the crossover month moves across those runs. If it moves
   a great deal, the decision rests on the assumptions rather than on the
   arithmetic, and that is worth knowing before anyone draws a conclusion.
