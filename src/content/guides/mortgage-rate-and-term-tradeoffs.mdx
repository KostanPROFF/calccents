A mortgage has two dials that move the payment, and they pull in opposite
directions once you look at the total cost. The rate and the term have to be
read together, because the effect of one depends on where the other is
set.

The
<a href="/tools/mortgage-payment/">mortgage payment calculator</a> has both
inputs for exactly this reason: change one and leave the other alone and the
result shows you the size of the trade.

## Rate and term move the payment together

**A lower rate lowers the payment.** Directly, and immediately, for every
payment on the loan.

**A longer term lowers the payment.** Also directly, and for the same
reason: the balance is paid down more slowly, so less interest is charged
each year.

Both of those lower the payment, and both of them raise the total cost.
That is the shape of the trade: the monthly number and the lifetime number
move in opposite directions.

## The numbers for a typical loan

Take $360,000 at 6.5 percent.

| Term | Monthly P&I | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| 30 years | $1,986 | about $355,000 |
| 20 years | $2,528 | about $246,000 |
| 15 years | $3,108 | about $201,000 |

Moving from 30 years to 15 years raises the payment by 56 percent and
removes about 43 percent of the interest. The asymmetry is the point: a
shortening of the term costs a lot of monthly payment and saves a moderate
amount of total interest.

## The effect of the rate is larger on a long loan

Change the rate on the same $360,000 and the effect depends on the term.

Over 30 years, moving from 6.5 percent to 7.5 percent changes the monthly
payment by about $90 and the total interest by roughly $60,000. Over 15
years, the same rate change moves the payment by about $110 and the total
interest by roughly $30,000.

The rate acts on the balance for as long as the balance is outstanding. A
long loan keeps the balance outstanding for twice as long, so the same rate
difference has twice as much room to work.

Which means the two dials are not equally powerful at every setting. On a
thirty-year loan, the rate dominates. On a fifteen-year loan, the term is
comparatively more important. The rule of thumb — a percentage point of
rate is worth more than five years of term — holds for the long loans that
most people take.

## Why the first decade decides the total

A 30-year loan spends its first decade with a balance that barely moves, and
that decade is where nearly all of the interest is charged. This is why the
term change above looks so dramatic: shortening the term does not merely
reduce the rate at which interest is charged, it removes years from the
front of the loan where the balance is highest.

It is also why an extra payment has so much effect. It reduces the balance
that the next month's interest is charged on, and it does it early, when the
balance is largest.

## Points, buydowns and the shape of the early loan

Two common features change the early payments without changing the
underlying loan.

A **discount point** is one percent of the loan amount paid at closing for a
lower rate. The trade has a break-even period, and the arithmetic is a
matter of comparing the fee against the difference in total interest over
the period the loan is kept.

A **rate buydown** reduces the rate for the first year or two and then
resets, usually to the index plus a margin. It changes the first payments
rather than the shape of the loan, and it is worth modelling separately
rather than as a rate.

## Reading the market context

Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey publishes the rate series most
often used as a reference point, and Fannie Mae's research pages carry the
equivalent commentary on the mortgage market. Neither is a quote for a
specific borrower.

The rates on this site are not connected to any market feed. The number you
enter is the number used, held constant for the whole term, which is what
makes the comparison between two rates a clean arithmetic comparison rather
than a forecast.

## The comparison worth running

Put the two options into the calculator with everything else identical, and
read four numbers: the monthly payment, the total interest, the total
housing cost and the payoff date. Then run each one with an extra payment,
because the term and the rate change how much an extra payment is worth.

That is the whole decision expressed as arithmetic. What it is worth in a
particular life is a separate question, and one that no calculator can
answer.
