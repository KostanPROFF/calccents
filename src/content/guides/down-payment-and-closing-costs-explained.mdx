Two sums of money leave your account before the mortgage does. The down
payment buys the house; closing costs buy the loan. Both change the
arithmetic from day one, and both are routinely estimated with a rule of
thumb rather than a figure.

## The down payment

A down payment is the part of the purchase price you pay yourself. The rest
is borrowed.

Its arithmetic effect is straightforward and its financial effect is not. A
larger down payment means a smaller loan, which means less interest charged
over the life of the loan. It also means less cash left in savings, which is
an effect the mortgage calculation does not capture.

The requirements come from the loan type rather than from arithmetic.
Conventional loans can be made with as little as 3 percent down, FHA loans
start at 3.5 percent, and VA loans can be made with no down payment at all.
Below 20 percent on a conventional loan, private mortgage insurance is
usually required, and it is a cost that sits outside the payment.

The HUD housing resources and the CFPB's mortgage tools both set out the
current programme requirements.

## What closing costs cover

Closing costs fall into a few groups:

**Third-party fees.** The title search, the title insurance, the appraisal,
the survey, the recording fees.

**Lender fees.** The origination charge for making the loan, and any
application or underwriting fee.

**Government charges.** Transfer taxes and recording fees, which are set by
state and county and can be a meaningful share of the total.

**Prepaid escrow.** Property tax and insurance for the period after
closing, collected up front because the bills have already been paid.

The last group is the one that surprises people most, because it is money
that looks like a cost but is really a prepayment of a later bill. If the
seller asks for six months of property tax at closing, you are not paying
six months of tax; you are paying for the months that have already been
billed.

## How they are calculated

Most closing costs are one of two things: a percentage of the purchase
price, or a flat fee. The origination charge is normally a percentage of the
loan amount.

That gives a useful approximation: on a $450,000 house with a $360,000
loan, costs of roughly 2 to 3 percent of the price and roughly 1 percent of
the loan are a reasonable place to start estimating. The spread is wide
because the components vary by state, by county and by the complexity of
the title.

The CFPB publishes a tool that works out the closing costs in a particular
county, and it is the most accurate way to get a local figure.

## The Loan Estimate

Three business days after you apply for a loan, the lender has to give you a
Loan Estimate. It is standardised, which means the format cannot vary, and
it is the document that makes the numbers comparable between lenders.

Two features of it matter for a comparison. It shows the cash to close at
the top of the first page, which is the sum of the down payment, the closing
costs and any prepaid items. And it shows, line by line, which fees can be
financed and which cannot.

A third business day before closing, you get a Closing Disclosure. It is
based on the actual figures rather than the estimates, and it has to be
compared with the Loan Estimate. Some figures are allowed to change by a
limited amount and others cannot change at all, and the document states
which is which.

## How this interacts with the calculators

The mortgage calculator on this site takes the amount borrowed rather than
the purchase price, which keeps the arithmetic simple and keeps the
comparison honest. To model a purchase, work out the loan amount first:

**loan amount = purchase price − down payment + financed closing costs**

Then run the calculator with that figure. The financed closing costs matter
here, because they raise the amount borrowed and therefore raise both the
payment and the total interest.

The rent versus buy calculator does the same thing, and it treats closing
costs as a percentage of the price by default, so that they appear in the
upfront outlay rather than disappearing.

## The number that is easy to forget

In a rent versus buy comparison, the down payment and the closing costs are
the two largest cash outflows on the buying side, and they arrive before the
first monthly payment does. A comparison that starts from the monthly
figures has already skipped them.
