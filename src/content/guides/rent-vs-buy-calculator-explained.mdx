Renting and buying are two different arrangements for the same underlying
thing: a roof over your head and a place to work. The
<a href="/tools/rent-vs-buy/">rent versus buy calculator</a> compares them on
cash and on equity, and stops there.

It does not produce a verdict, and the reason is worth stating before the
arithmetic. A verdict would depend on facts the page cannot see — what else
you have saved, how stable your income is, how long you plan to be in the
place, how much you would mind a bad month. Those inputs change the answer
without changing any of the arithmetic.

## Two ledgers, one month at a time

The comparison runs two cash-flow ledgers side by side.

The **renting ledger** adds your monthly rent and your renter's insurance.
That is the whole of it. There is no deposit to recover, no maintenance, no
transaction cost at the end.

The **owning ledger** adds:

- The mortgage payment, taken from the standard amortization formula
- Property tax, as a percentage of the purchase price per year
- Homeowners insurance, as an annual figure divided by twelve
- Any HOA or condo fee
- A maintenance allowance, as a percentage of the purchase price per year
- The down payment and closing costs, on the first month

The maintenance allowance is the one people argue about. Repairs do not
appear on a closing statement, but they arrive, and they arrive whether or not
a mortgage is attached. Excluding them flatters owning; treating them as
enormous flatters it the other way. Expressing them as a percentage of the
purchase price each year keeps them next to the other running costs, where
they can be argued about properly.

## Equity is the part that is easy to miss

Running the cash ledgers on their own produces an incomplete picture, because
owning puts an asset on the balance sheet while renting does not.

So the calculator tracks two more figures each month:

- **Home value**, grown or shrunk monthly at the annual rate you enter
- **Remaining loan balance**, taken from the amortization schedule

Their difference is your equity:

**equity = home value − remaining loan balance**

The **net cost of owning** is then the cash you have paid, minus that equity:

**net cost = down payment + closing costs + all monthly ownership costs − equity**

This is the number that can be compared with the rent you would have paid
over the same period. Early on, the net cost of owning is high, because you
have paid a large upfront sum and built very little equity. As the years pass
and the balance falls, the net cost drops, and at some point it falls below
the rent.

## The crossover month

That point is the crossover month, and it is the most useful single number
the tool produces. It is the first month where:

**net cost of owning < total rent paid over the same months**

Read it carefully. Before the crossover, renting has cost less in cash
terms. After it, owning has cost less once equity is counted. Neither side of
that line is a recommendation; it is the point where the two lines on the
graph cross, and the location of that crossing depends entirely on the
inputs.

The crossover gets later when the rent is low relative to the purchase
price, when the rate is high, when the horizon is short, and when the
appreciation assumption is low or negative. It never arrives at all when
rent is small relative to the purchase price, which is the arithmetic reason
a cash purchase in an expensive market can be the more expensive option
over five years.

## What is deliberately outside

The comparison covers cash and equity. It does not cover:

- **Tax on a sale.** Any gain and the exclusion that may apply to it are
  outside the scope of the tool.
- **The mortgage interest deduction.** Whether that is worth anything depends
  on your marginal rate and your circumstances.
- **Transaction costs on the purchase.** Closing costs enter as a percentage
  of the price; the detail of what they cover is in the guide on closing
  costs.
- **Different quality of housing.** A rental is not the same building as a
  purchase in a different neighbourhood, and no formula captures that.
- **The option to move.** Renting keeps a lease; buying keeps a mortgage. Both
  have their own costs of exit, and neither appears here.

## A note on the appreciation input

The annual change in value you enter is applied every month for the whole
horizon, in both directions if the number is negative. No forecast is
involved, and no historical average is substituted for your input.

That matters, because the assumption is one of the most powerful levers in
the whole comparison. A home price that rises at 3 percent a year and one that
rises at 1 percent produce materially different equity figures over a
five-year horizon, and both are plausible assumptions rather than facts about
the future.

The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis publishes long-run house price series
that are useful for calibrating the input, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics
publishes the inflation data behind the real-value adjustments elsewhere on
this site. Neither is a prediction.
