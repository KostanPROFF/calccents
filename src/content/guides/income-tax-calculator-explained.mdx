The federal income tax on a year of earnings is a published table applied to a
number that is assembled in a few steps. The
<a href="/tools/income-tax/">income tax calculator</a> on this site performs
those steps in order and shows the working. The 2026 figures it uses come from
Rev. Proc. 2025-32, published in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2025-45 on
3 November 2025.

Nothing on the page is a forecast and nothing is a live figure. The brackets
and the standard deduction are the published ones for the 2026 tax year, and
every number in the result follows from them and from the values entered.

## From gross pay to taxable income

Taxable income is not the number on the payslip. The calculator starts from
gross income for the year, subtracts the pre-tax deductions entered there —
a 401(k) contribution, an HSA or an FSA — and then subtracts the deduction.

The deduction is the standard deduction for the filing status, unless an
itemised figure larger than it is entered. For 2026 the standard deduction is
$16,100 for a single filer and for married filing separately, $32,200 for
married filing jointly, and $24,150 for head of household.

What remains is taxable income. The brackets apply to that number, and the
state estimate applies to the same number.

## How the brackets work

The IRS publishes each bracket as a fixed amount of tax plus a percentage of
the excess over the bottom of that bracket, rather than as a flat rate on
each slice. For a single filer in 2026, the rates run 10 percent on the first
$12,400, 12 percent from there to $50,400, 22 percent to $105,700, 24 percent
to $201,775, 32 percent to $256,225, 35 percent to $640,600, and 37 percent
on anything above that.

In that form the table reads as a list of checkpoints: the tax is $1,240 at
$12,400, $5,800 at $50,400, $17,966 at $105,700, $41,024 at $201,775, $58,448
at $256,225, and $192,979.25 at $640,600. A taxable income of $83,900 for a
single filer therefore gives $5,800 plus 22 percent of the $33,500 above
$50,400, which is $5,800 plus $7,370 — a federal tax of $13,170.

Two rates come out of that, and they answer different questions. The marginal
rate is the rate the next dollar meets, so at $83,900 of taxable income the
next dollar is taxed at 22 percent. The effective rate is the whole tax
divided by the whole income: the same reader, with gross income of $100,000
and the standard deduction of $16,100, pays $13,170 in federal tax, which is
about 13 percent of what they earned.

## The standard deduction and itemising

A dollar of deduction is a dollar of taxable income, and taxable income is
taxed at the marginal rate, so a dollar of deduction saves the marginal rate
in tax. The size of the deduction therefore matters more to a reader in a
higher bracket than to a reader in a lower one: the first dollars of income
are taxed at 10 percent, so the standard deduction is worth $16,100 of tax to
a single filer at the bottom of the table and considerably more further up.

The calculator asks for the itemised deduction and uses it when it is larger
than the standard deduction. Where the itemised figure is smaller, the
standard deduction stands and taxable income is higher. The two numbers in
the result show which one was used.

## What the figure does not cover

The federal figure is ordinary income tax on taxable income, and the
calculation stops there. It does not model:

- **Tax credits.** Credits reduce the tax after it is computed, and no credit
  is applied here.
- **The child tax credit.** The same applies to it, and it is one of the
  largest differences between a tax liability and a tax bill.
- **Capital gains rates.** The ordinary income rates are applied to the whole
  of taxable income, with no separate treatment for gains.
- **The senior deduction.** The additional amount available to older filers is
  not part of the calculation.

State income tax is handled separately and is deliberately simple. The rate
is the one typed into the calculator, and it is applied to the whole of
taxable income. Most states use progressive rates, so a single percentage on
the whole base overstates the tax in a progressive state and is exact in a
flat-rate one. The state figure is an estimate that moves with the rate
entered.

Payroll taxes are outside this calculation as well. Social Security and
Medicare are withheld from wages under their own rates and ceilings, which
belong to the net pay calculation rather than to income tax on earnings.

Every figure in the result follows from the published 2026 table and from the
values on the page. Change the income, the filing status, the deduction or
the state rate, and the tax, the effective rate and the income left over all
move with them.
