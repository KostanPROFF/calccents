A mortgage payment is one number with four parts, and the reason two
people can quote different payments for the same house is usually that they
are counting different parts.

The <a href="/tools/mortgage-payment/">mortgage payment calculator</a> on this
site splits those parts apart. The principal and interest figure comes from
arithmetic. Property tax, insurance and any HOA fee come from you, because the
calculator has no way of knowing what your assessor or your insurer charges.
Showing them separately is more useful than showing a single total, because the
total is only as good as its least visible component.

## The payment formula

The scheduled monthly payment is the standard amortization formula:

**M = P · r(1 + r)ⁿ / ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)**

Where:

- **P** is the amount borrowed, not the price of the house. With a $450,000
  house and 20 percent down, P is $360,000.
- **r** is the annual interest rate divided by twelve. A rate of 6.5 percent
  becomes 0.0054166… per month.
- **n** is the number of monthly payments. A 30-year term is 360 payments.

The formula is worth reading once, because it explains something that
otherwise looks arbitrary: the payment does not depend on how much you
borrow, in proportion to how much you borrow. Borrow twice as much and you
pay roughly twice as much, not four times as much. What changes with the size
of the loan is the interest total, which grows faster than the payment does.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau sets out the same formula in its
mortgage guidance, and the widely used amortization tables in the industry
are built from it.

## Why the payment stays level while the split changes

The formula produces a level payment. What is not level is what the payment
is made of. In month one, almost all of it is interest, because the balance
is still the full amount borrowed. In the last month, almost all of it is
principal, because the balance has been ground down to almost nothing.

The mechanics are simple. Each month:

1. Interest is charged on the balance outstanding at the start of the month.
2. The payment covers that interest first.
3. Whatever is left over reduces the balance.
4. Next month's interest is charged on the smaller balance.

The consequence is that a 30-year loan is repaid much faster than it feels.
A large share of the balance is still outstanding at the five-year mark, and
a meaningful share of the total interest has already been paid by then. This
is the single most counter-intuitive fact about mortgages, and it is the
reason the year-by-year amortization table is worth reading once.

## What PITI adds

The payment lenders collect is usually PITI:

- **Principal** — the part that repays the loan.
- **Interest** — the cost of borrowing it.
- **Taxes** — property tax, usually collected monthly into escrow.
- **Insurance** — homeowners insurance, also usually collected into escrow.

Tax and insurance are annual amounts divided by twelve for display. The
division is a timing convenience, not a discount: over a year you pay the
same total either way. What changes month to month is which month a tax bill
lands in, and escrow is designed to smooth that out.

HOA fees sit outside PITI but inside the monthly outflow for a condo or a
house in a planned development. They are entered separately for the same
reason: the number depends entirely on the property.

## Extra payments

An extra payment applied to principal lowers the balance that the next
month's interest is charged on. The scheduled payment does not change; the
loan simply finishes sooner, and less interest is paid in total.

This is worth understanding mechanically rather than in the abstract. A
$200 extra payment on a $360,000 loan at 6.5 percent is not $200 of
interest saved — it is $200 of principal removed, and then every subsequent
month's interest is charged on a balance that is $200 lower, and then
$200 lower again. The saving compounds in the same way the loan does.

## What the calculation leaves out

A payment is one part of the cost of a home. The calculator deliberately
excludes:

- **Mortgage insurance**, which depends on the loan-to-value ratio and the
  credit behind the loan, and is cancelled on its own schedule.
- **Points and origination fees**, which are paid at closing and can be
  folded into the rate for comparison, but are not part of the payment.
- **Closing costs**, which are the subject of a separate guide.
- **Escrow timing**, which decides which month bears a larger instalment.
- **Inflation**, which changes what the payment means relative to your
  income, but not what it is.

Each of those is a real number. Keeping them out of the payment figure is
what lets the payment figure be checked against a published table.

## Checking the result

If you want to confirm a figure independently, take the amount borrowed, the
rate and the number of payments, and work the formula out on paper or with a
spreadsheet. If the calculator's answer and yours agree, both are probably
right. If they disagree, the most likely causes are a different definition of
the loan amount, a rate entered as a decimal rather than a percentage, or a
term in years where the formula wants months.

That check is the whole point of showing the working.
