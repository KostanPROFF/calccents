Simple interest and compound interest are the same idea computed two
different ways, and the difference between them is one of the largest gaps
in ordinary arithmetic.

Simple interest charges interest on the original amount. Compound interest
charges interest on the original amount plus the interest already credited.
That is the whole difference.

## The same numbers, two answers

Take $10,000 at 7 percent over 30 years.

**Simple interest** pays 7 percent of the original $10,000 every year: $700
a year, unchanged, for thirty years. The final balance is $31,000.

**Compound interest**, compounded annually, starts at $10,000 and grows. The
final balance is $76,123.

The gap is $45,123 on a $10,000 starting balance, and it comes entirely from
the fact that compound interest is charged on a growing base.

## Why the gap widens rather than stays constant

The growth is not linear. In a simple-interest account, each year adds the
same amount. In a compound-interest account, each year adds slightly more
than the year before, because the base has grown.

Three effects are worth separating, because they behave differently:

**The interest on interest.** The largest effect, and the one that requires
the longest time to become significant. Over a year it is barely visible;
over thirty years it is most of the balance.

**The contribution effect.** A regular monthly contribution is itself a
form of compounding, because each contribution earns interest for the rest
of the period. The contribution made in month one earns interest for the
whole horizon; the one made in the last month earns almost none. This is
why the tool lets you switch the contribution between the start and the end
of the month.

**The frequency effect.** Same nominal rate, more frequent compounding, more
money. The effective annual rate is what makes this visible: 7 percent
compounded monthly is 7.229 percent, and 7.229 percent is the rate you are
actually earning.

## The doubling approximation

The rule of 70 is a shortcut: divide 70 by the annual rate to get the years
it takes for money to double. At 7 percent that is ten years; at 3 percent,
23 years.

It is an approximation, and the reason it is needed at all is that the exact
doubling time depends on the compounding frequency. The rule of 70 assumes
annual compounding. With monthly compounding, 7 percent doubles a little
sooner than ten years.

The calculator is the way to check it. Run thirty years at 7 percent with no
contributions, and compare the final balance with twice the starting
balance. The approximation is close; the exact figure is on the screen.

## Where the same mechanism runs the other way

Compound interest is not a favour granted by finance. It is a property of
the arithmetic, and it applies to balances owed as well as balances earned.

On a revolving credit card balance, the interest is charged on the balance
including previous interest. Paying only the minimum on a card is the
compound interest calculation running in reverse, with a rate that is high
enough to make the effect quick.

The same is true of unpaid interest on some other arrangements. What
determines the direction is only which side of the balance you are on.

## A note on the rates involved

The Federal Reserve publishes the H.15 selected interest rates, which is the
reference series for what savings products have actually paid. The Internal
Revenue Service publishes the rules for the accounts where growth is
deferred, and the Investor.gov glossary defines the terms used here.

None of those is a forecast of what a rate will be. The calculator uses
whatever rate you enter, held constant for the whole horizon, because a
projection that changes the rate according to some assumed future path is a
different kind of claim from arithmetic.
