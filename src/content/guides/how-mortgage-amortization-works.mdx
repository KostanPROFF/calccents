Amortization is the process by which a mortgage is repaid, and it produces
one of the most counter-intuitive results in personal finance: a thirty-year
loan is not thirty years of paying it off. It is about a decade of paying it
off, followed by two decades of paying for the privilege of having paid it
off.

The mechanism is simple enough to hold in your head, which is worth doing
before looking at any schedule.

## Interest first, always

Every mortgage payment has two parts, and the order in which they are applied
is fixed by the contract:

1. Interest is charged on the balance outstanding at the start of the month.
2. The payment is applied to that interest.
3. Whatever remains of the payment reduces the principal.

The balance for the next month is the balance for this month, less the
principal that was paid. That is the entire rule.

The consequence follows immediately. In month one, the balance is the full
amount borrowed, so the interest charge is at its largest, and the payment —
which was set to cover that interest plus a small slice of principal — is
almost entirely interest. As the balance falls, the interest charge falls
with it, and the same payment starts covering more principal.

## The arithmetic of a single month

The interest charge for a month is:

**interest = balance × (annual rate ÷ 12)**

Take a $360,000 loan at 6.5 percent. The monthly rate is 0.54167 percent.
In the first month, the interest is $360,000 × 0.0054167 = $1,950. The
scheduled payment on a 30-year term is $1,986. The difference, $36, is what
reduces the principal in the first month. It is a rounding error against the
balance.

A year later, the balance has fallen to roughly $357,500. The interest
charge is now about $1,936, and the principal portion has grown to about
$50. Three years in, the principal portion is around $80 a month. It is still
small. It is also growing, and the growth accelerates, which is the whole
story.

## The two curves

A useful way to picture a mortgage is as two curves moving against each
other. The balance curve starts high and falls slowly at first, then steeply,
then flattens as it approaches zero. The cumulative interest curve starts at
zero, rises quickly, and then rises more slowly, because the interest charge
is applied to a shrinking balance.

The first curve is the one people think about. The second is the one that
determines the total cost, and the gap between them is the total interest.

At the five-year mark on a typical 30-year loan, a little under a fifth of
the balance is gone and roughly a quarter of the total interest has already
been paid. At the fifteen-year mark, most of the balance is gone. This is
why the exact year matters: "how much have I paid off" and "how much have I
paid in interest" are very different questions, and the amortization table
answers both.

## Why the last payment is different

A monthly payment has to be a fixed number of cents, and a loan with an
interest rate and a term of whole months does not divide evenly. The
rounding leaves a small balance outstanding at the end.

The final payment is adjusted to clear it. This is not a special feature of
any lender; it is arithmetic. The <a href="/tools/mortgage-payment/">mortgage
payment calculator</a> on this site does the same thing, and the adjustment
is visible in the last row of the schedule.

## The accelerated path

The amortization schedule is also the argument for extra payments, and the
argument is mechanical rather than motivational. An extra payment reduces
the principal. A smaller principal means a smaller interest charge next
month, which means more of the next payment is available for principal. The
effect compounds in exactly the same way the loan does.

The scheduled payment does not change. The loan simply ends sooner, and the
interest that would have been charged on a balance that no longer exists is
never charged. The total interest figure in the calculator drops as the extra
payment rises, and the payoff date moves forward.

## What amortization does not cover

The schedule covers the loan. It does not cover property tax, insurance,
HOA fees, maintenance, or the effect of inflation on a payment that runs for
three decades. Those are running costs rather than loan costs, and they are
handled separately in the guides on property tax and on the total cost of a
mortgage.
