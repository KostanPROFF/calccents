Two of the four parts of a mortgage payment are not set by the loan at all.
Property tax comes from a local government and homeowners insurance comes
from an insurer, and both are estimates until the first bill arrives.

The <a href="/tools/mortgage-payment/">mortgage payment calculator</a> takes
both as inputs, because a national average is a starting point at best. Here
is where each number actually comes from.

## Property tax

Property tax is an annual amount calculated as a rate applied to an assessed
value. The structure is simple; the complications are all in the two
variables.

**The rate** is set locally, expressed in mills or as a percentage of
assessed value. It varies enormously between jurisdictions. A millage in one
state can be several times the millage in another, and within a single
county it can differ by a factor of two or more between neighbouring
townships.

**The assessed value** is not the purchase price. This is the detail that
catches people out on their first bill. Many jurisdictions cap the assessed
value at a percentage of the price, others use a separate mass appraisal
formula, and some reassess on a cycle. The result is that the first tax bill
is often lower than price times a rate, and that the figure rises later as
reassessments catch up.

Property tax is also re-rated periodically. Rates can change between years,
and a reassessment can change the value the rate is applied to without the
house changing at all.

The HUD and CFPB housing resources both describe this structure, and they
are the right place to look for how a specific jurisdiction works.

## Homeowners insurance

The premium is built from several inputs, and the largest is one people
often miss: the **replacement cost** of the structure.

Replacement cost is what it would cost to rebuild the house, not what the
house is worth or what it cost. If construction costs rise faster than house
prices, the replacement cost rises faster than the purchase price, and so
does the premium. That is one reason insurance is a cost that does not track
the property's market value.

The other inputs are the location (weather, fire risk, crime rates, and the
cost of claims in that area), the deductible, the coverage limits, and the
claims history of the address.

Two consequences are worth knowing before a purchase. A rebuild can raise the
replacement cost and therefore the premium. And coverage limits set at the
original purchase price can become inadequate after a rebuild, which turns
into a shortfall at claim time.

## How to get better estimates

The order that works best is short:

1. **The tax assessor.** The local assessor's office publishes the assessed
   value and the applicable rate for a specific address. That gives an
   estimate grounded in the actual jurisdiction rather than in a national
   average.
2. **An insurance quote for the specific address.** Quotes are free, and the
   number is produced by the model that will actually price the policy. Ask
   for the replacement cost figure, not only the premium.
3. **The lender's escrow estimate.** Once a loan is in place, the lender
   computes an escrow payment based on its own figures. It is not a promise
   for the following year, and it will move when the tax bill and the
   insurance renewal arrive.

## Why the calculator asks for these separately

Tax and insurance are annual amounts, and the monthly payment shows them
divided by twelve. The division is a timing convenience, not a discount:
over a year the same total is paid either way. What changes month to month
is which month a tax bill lands in, and escrow exists to smooth that out.

Separating them in the calculator also makes the running cost visible. A
payment that is 60 percent principal and interest and 40 percent tax and
insurance behaves very differently from one that is 90 percent principal and
interest, and the two are not comparable without that split.

## What changes over time

Both figures are estimates at the moment you make them, and both are subject
to revision. The inflation data published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics
is the reference for the construction costs behind replacement cost, and the
tax rate is a matter of local policy. Neither is fixed for the life of a
thirty-year loan.
