Two accounts that both advertise "7 percent" are not necessarily the same
account. The nominal rate is the number, and the compounding frequency
decides what the number turns into.

The difference over a year is small. The difference over thirty years is not,
and the arithmetic behind it is worth understanding rather than estimating.

## Why the frequency matters

The mechanism is a timing effect. Interest credited monthly is credited
earlier than interest credited annually, and interest that has been credited
earlier has had longer to earn interest itself.

Concretely: with annual compounding, interest for the whole year lands on
one date at the end. With monthly compounding, a twelfth of the annual
interest lands at the end of every month and starts earning interest
immediately. The extra interest is small, but it is earned for a long time.

## The effective annual rate

The way to compare two frequencies is the effective annual rate: the rate
that produces the same result over a year as the stated rate compounded at
the stated frequency.

**EAR = (1 + r)ⁿ − 1**

Where **r** is the nominal annual rate divided by the number of periods per
year, and **n** is the number of periods per year.

At 7 percent:

| Frequency | Effective annual rate |
|---|---|
| Annually | 7.000% |
| Twice a year | 7.122% |
| Every four months | 7.165% |
| Quarterly | 7.186% |
| Every two months | 7.207% |
| Monthly | 7.229% |

The sequence is monotonic, and the steps get smaller as the frequency
increases, which is the practical point: the first doubling of the
frequency is worth more than the last.

The
<a href="/tools/compound-interest/">compound interest calculator</a>
computes the effective rate directly, and it is shown in the results next to
the nominal rate so the two can be compared.

## How much difference does it make

The useful way to look at this is to compare the total, not the rate.

On $10,000 at 7 percent over one year, monthly compounding produces $10,722.90
against $10,700.00 compounded annually. The difference is $22.90, which is
not worth a decision on its own.

Over thirty years with no further contributions, the same $10,000 becomes
$76,123 compounded monthly and $75,624 compounded annually — a difference of
$499. With a monthly contribution of $500 over the same period, both the
balances and the difference grow much larger, because the contributions are
earning interest at the higher effective rate as well.

The pattern is consistent: the gap between two frequencies is small in the
early years and widens steadily, because it is itself being compounded.

## Why this tool offers six frequencies

The tool offers annually, twice a year, every four months, quarterly, every
two months and monthly. The reason is that the contributions here are made
monthly, and the calculation keeps the compounding period and the
contribution period identical.

Any frequency that divides twelve keeps that arithmetic exact rather than
approximate. Frequencies that do not divide twelve would require splitting
a contribution across two compounding periods, which introduces a rounding
convention that is an artefact of the model rather than a feature of the
product.

## What this is not

This is a comparison of two credit terms, not a comparison of two products.
Two accounts with the same nominal rate and the same frequency can still be
very different, because of fees, minimum balances, limits on withdrawals,
and how the account is treated for tax.

The Investor.gov pages on fees and expenses and on account types describe
those differences. The Federal Reserve's H.15 series records what rates
savings products have actually paid, which is useful for checking whether a
quoted rate is in a plausible range.

## A practical test

Take a rate from the H.15 series, put it into the calculator at annual
compounding and then at monthly compounding, and read the difference in the
final balance. That difference is the value of the more frequent credit, and
it is a number rather than a rule of thumb.
