Compound interest is one of the few ideas in finance that is genuinely
simple to state and genuinely hard to feel. The
<a href="/tools/compound-interest/">compound interest calculator</a> on this
site exists to make the arithmetic visible, because the arithmetic is what
distinguishes this from a rule of thumb.

The rule of thumb says: interest is interest on the interest. The arithmetic
says: here is what that does to your balance, month by month, at the rate you
entered.

## What the tool does

You give it a starting balance, a monthly contribution, an annual rate, a
number of years, a compounding frequency, and an inflation rate. It advances
one compounding period at a time.

In each period it adds the interest earned on the current balance, and then
adds the contribution for that period — at the start of the period or the end
of it, depending on the setting. It repeats until the horizon is reached.

The order matters. A contribution made at the start of a period earns
interest for that whole period, so it ends with more than the same
contribution made at the end. The difference per payment is small. The
difference over thirty years is not.

## The closed form

The same result can be written as a single expression:

**FV = P(1 + r)ⁿ + PMT · ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1) / r**

Where **P** is the starting balance, **r** is the annual rate divided by the
number of compounding periods per year, **n** is the number of periods, and
**PMT** is the contribution made in each period.

When contributions are made at the start of each period, the contribution
term is multiplied by (1 + r), because those contributions earn an extra
period of interest.

The calculator runs the periods in sequence rather than evaluating this
expression once, and the reason is the year-by-year table: to produce a
table you need each intermediate balance, and the sequence produces them for
free. The two methods agree to the cent, which is itself a useful check —
the calculation engine is tested against exactly that agreement.

At a zero rate the formula degenerates, because dividing by r is undefined.
The calculation handles that case explicitly rather than letting it produce
a division by zero: with no interest, the balance grows by the deposits
alone, which is exactly what happens.

## Nominal, effective and real

Three rates appear in the results, and they are easy to confuse.

The **nominal rate** is the number on the account: 7 percent. It is the
number that gets quoted, and it is what you type in.

The **effective annual rate** is what the balance actually earns over a year
once compounding is applied: (1 + r) raised to the number of periods per year,
minus one. For 7 percent compounded monthly that is 7.229 percent. The
investor's regulator explains the distinction in its glossary, and the
difference between the two is a compounding effect that is worth money over
long horizons.

The **real rate** removes inflation from the rate the money actually earns:

**real rate = (1 + effective annual rate) / (1 + inflation) − 1**

This is the rate that describes how much purchasing power the balance holds.
The nominal rate is not the number to use here: 7 percent is what the account
quotes, and what lands in the balance over a year is 7.229 percent once the
compounding is applied. At 7 percent compounded monthly and 2.5 percent
inflation, the real rate is about 4.61 percent. The balance in the
calculator's real-value column is the nominal balance divided by inflation
compounded over the same period.

## The frequency question

Compounding more often, at the same nominal rate, produces a larger balance.
The nominal rate does not change; the effective rate does, and the effective
rate is what the compounding does.

The tool offers six frequencies, and they are exactly the ones that divide
twelve. The restriction is not arbitrary: the contributions are monthly, and
keeping the compounding period and the contribution period identical keeps
the arithmetic exact. Monthly compounding against monthly deposits is the
common case, and the others cover accounts that credit quarterly, twice a
year or annually.

## What this projection leaves out

- **Fees.** An account charge is usually a percentage of the balance, so it
  compounds too. Investor.gov's pages on fees and expenses explain the
  structures in detail.
- **Taxes.** The treatment of a retirement account and of investment gains
  depends on the account type and on the person. The Internal Revenue
  Service publishes the rules for the accounts it administers.
- **Volatility.** A real investment balance falls in years when markets fall.
  A fixed rate cannot represent that, and the tool does not try.
- **Contribution changes.** Raising a contribution over time, or stopping
  part-way, is outside the model. The contribution is constant across the
  horizon.

## A rule of thumb, checked

The rule of thumb is that money doubles roughly every so many years at a
given rate. You can check that against the tool. Run 30 years at 7 percent
with no contributions and compare the result with the starting balance; run
the same calculation with a contribution and watch the doubling time change.
The tool exists so that the rule of thumb can be tested rather than
believed.
