Extra mortgage payments get described in two ways. One says they save
thousands of dollars. The other says they are a waste. Both are describing
the same arithmetic, and the arithmetic is worth looking at directly.

The <a href="/tools/mortgage-payment/">mortgage payment calculator</a> has an
extra payment field for exactly this reason: the effect is easy to see once
it is computed rather than asserted.

## The mechanism

A mortgage payment covers interest first and principal second. That rule does
not change when you pay more.

So when you add $200 to a scheduled payment:

1. The scheduled portion still covers that month's interest and the same
   principal slice it always did.
2. The extra $200 goes entirely to principal.
3. The balance is now $200 lower.
4. Next month, the interest is charged on a balance that is $200 lower, so
   the interest charge is $200 × (monthly rate) lower.
5. That small saving is now available for principal, because the scheduled
   payment is unchanged.

Step four is where the compounding lives. The saving is small per month and
it is permanent, and it repeats. Over a long enough horizon, extra payments
remove more interest than the extra cash would have paid directly, even
though the extra cash is what caused the difference.

## Why the payment does not change

This surprises people. The scheduled payment stays exactly as it was, because
the amortization schedule was built around that number. What changes is the
number of payments. The loan ends sooner.

The effect on the monthly budget is therefore a temporary one: the extra
amount is paid for as long as the loan lasts, and then stops. The payoff
date in the calculator's results is the direct measure of how long that
lasts.

## Reading the payoff date

The payoff date is the number to watch, and it moves in a way that is easy
to misjudge.

An extra payment that looks large relative to the loan amount can move the
payoff date only a few months. The reason is that a loan is already
front-loaded with interest: most of the interest has been paid by the time
the balance is finally paid off, and shortening the tail removes less than
the proportional share of the loan suggests.

The same extra payment applied to a shorter-term loan does more, because
more of that loan's interest is still ahead of it. The same extra payment
applied early does more than the same payment applied late, because the
balance it reduces is larger at the start.

The calculator lets you change the extra payment and watch the payoff date
and the total interest move. Running it at $0, $100, $200 and $500 is a
faster way to understand the shape of the effect than any rule of thumb.

## What it does not tell you

The extra payment calculation is about the loan and nothing else. It does
not model:

- **The alternative use of the money.** Whether $200 a month is better spent
  reducing a loan or sitting in an account is a question about returns, and
  the arithmetic of the loan does not answer it.
- **Tax treatment.** Interest and principal are treated differently for tax
  purposes in many situations, and that difference is not in the calculation.
- **Prepayment penalties.** Most conventional mortgages do not carry them,
  and the calculation does not include any.
- **Changes of plans.** A loan that is paid off early releases cash flow
  earlier, and what that cash flow is then used for is outside the model.

## A note on how lenders apply the payment

The calculation assumes the extra amount goes to principal, which is the
standard treatment. The mechanics of an unscheduled principal payment are
governed by the note and the servicing rules that go with it, and those are
worth reading before making a large one-off payment. The behaviour in the
calculator matches the common case, and the payoff date it reports is the
month the balance reaches zero.
