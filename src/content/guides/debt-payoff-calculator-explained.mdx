Several debts at once do not add up the way a single balance does. A card at
18% and a personal loan at 7% both take a payment every month, and the order
in which those payments are applied changes what the same money costs in
interest. The <a href="/tools/debt-payoff/">debt payoff calculator</a> runs
that comparison directly.

## The month-by-month model

The calculator simulates the debts one month at a time, and the steps of the
simulation are the same every month.

Interest for the month is charged on the balance at the start of the month and
is added to that balance. A $10,000 balance at 18% carries $150 of interest in
the month, and the balance to work against is $10,150.

The money available that month is the sum of every minimum payment plus
whatever extra payment was entered. Every debt that still has a balance takes
its own minimum payment, and that payment covers its own interest first and
its principal second. What is left of the month's money — the extra payment,
and the minimums of debts that have already been cleared — goes to the debt
the chosen order points at.

When a debt reaches zero it stops taking a minimum payment, and that minimum
joins the money available for the debts that are still open. A $200 minimum
that becomes $200 of extra principal is what makes a payoff order accelerate
rather than merely rearrange the payments.

A single debt shows the same arithmetic in its simplest form. A $10,000
balance at 18% with a $200 minimum payment is cleared in 94 months, and about
$8,622 of interest is paid along the way. The balance falls by $50 in the
first month, because $150 of interest is added and $200 goes out, and by a
little more in each month after that, because the interest is charged on a
balance that is going down.

## Avalanche and snowball

The question the calculator asks is which debt the extra money goes to first.

The avalanche order starts with the highest interest rate. The debt that
costs the most to carry is paid down first, and that is where the smaller
total interest comes from.

The snowball order starts with the smallest balance. The smallest debt is
cleared first and its minimum payment joins the monthly budget sooner, so the
first account reaches zero earlier even though more interest is paid overall.

The two orders make different things visible. One shows the smallest number
of dollars at the end of the run, the other shows the first balance gone.

## What the comparison shows

The calculator runs three simulations and places them next to each other.

The first is the minimums-only run: every minimum payment and no extra money.
It is the baseline the other two are measured against.

The second is the avalanche run with the extra payment added. The third is the
snowball run with the same extra payment added. For each one the calculator
reports the months until every debt is cleared, the total interest paid, and
the interest saved against the minimums-only baseline.

Those two savings figures are the comparison the tool exists to produce, and
they answer different questions. The avalanche figure is the smaller of the
two, and the snowball figure is the larger. What sits between them is the
price of clearing the smallest account first, expressed in dollars of interest.

The gap depends on the numbers entered. With a single debt the two orders
are identical. With debts of similar rates and similar balances the two
orders come out close to each other. The gap widens when a large balance
carries a high rate and a small one carries a low rate.

There is one case where the baseline does not exist. When the minimum payments
are smaller than the interest the debts charge in a month, the balance does not
fall: the interest is added, the payment covers part of it, and what is left
grows the balance again. On the minimums alone the debts are never cleared,
so there is no finished schedule to measure the other two runs against. The
calculator leaves the interest-saved figures empty and says so on the page
rather than printing a number made of a hundred years of interest that never
ends. The avalanche and snowball runs still finish as long as the extra
payment covers the interest, and their months and total interest are the
figures that describe the debts. Where the extra payment does not cover it
either, those runs report no months and no interest for the same reason.

## What the model leaves out

The model is monthly and plain, and several things sit outside it.

Variable interest rates are held at the level that was entered for every month
of the run.

Late fees and balance-transfer fees are not part of the arithmetic.

Credit card interest is often accrued daily and compounded daily, while the
calculator applies monthly accrual, so the cost of a card balance comes out
lower than it is billed.

Interest on some debts is tax deductible, and that tax effect is not in the
calculation.

A payment that does not arrive stops the simulation rather than being carried
as a delinquency, so the figures describe the schedule as it was scheduled
rather than as it turned out.
