A mortgage offer is usually presented as a monthly number, and the monthly
number is a poor way to compare two offers. It is a good way to check that
you can afford something, and a bad way to work out which one costs less.

The reason is that a monthly payment is a function of three inputs, and
changing any one of them changes the payment while leaving the loan's total
cost moving in the opposite direction.

## Why the payment is a misleading number

Consider two loans on the same house, at the same rate:

- **Loan A**: $360,000 over 30 years at 6.5 percent — about $1,986 a month
- **Loan B**: $360,000 over 15 years at 6.5 percent — about $3,108 a month

Loan B costs $1,122 more every month. Over the life of the loan, Loan A costs
roughly $355,000 more in interest. The shorter loan buys a monthly payment
that is 57 percent higher, and in exchange the total interest falls by a
little over half.

Neither is better in the abstract. They are better for different
circumstances, and the circumstances are not arithmetic. But the two facts
above are arithmetic, and they are the facts a comparison needs.

## The four numbers worth comparing

A mortgage comparison is clearer with four numbers rather than one:

1. **Monthly principal and interest** — what leaves your account each month.
2. **Total interest** — the cost of the loan, over its whole life.
3. **Total housing cost** — everything you pay to live in the property over
   the life of the loan: principal, interest, tax, insurance and HOA.
4. **Payoff date** — when the loan is actually finished.

The <a href="/tools/mortgage-payment/">mortgage payment calculator</a>
reports all four. The payoff date is the one that is most often left out,
and it is the one that shows the effect of a term change most clearly.

## The trade-off a term change makes

A longer term lowers the payment and raises the total. The mechanism is
simple: the balance stays higher for longer, and interest is charged on the
balance.

The trade is not linear. Moving from 15 years to 30 years more than doubles
the payment. Moving from 30 years to 40 years does not double anything, and
it increases the total interest substantially, because the balance is higher
for a decade longer. The first decade of a mortgage is where nearly all of
the interest is charged, and a term that extends that decade is expensive in
a way that the payment alone does not show.

## What the rate does to the same comparison

The rate has a similar shape of effect. On the same $360,000 over 30 years,
moving from 6.5 percent to 7.5 percent changes the monthly payment by about
$90 and the total interest by roughly $60,000. A rate difference of one
percentage point sounds small and is not, over thirty years, because it
applies to a balance that stays high.

This is why rate comparisons use total figures. The Freddie Mac Primary
Mortgage Market Survey publishes the rate series most often used as a
reference point, and Fannie Mae's research pages carry the equivalent
commentary.

## What belongs in the comparison and what does not

Some costs belong in a mortgage comparison and some do not.

**In the comparison:** principal, interest, property tax, insurance, HOA,
and the cost of the money used for the down payment, because it is not
available for anything else while it sits in the house.

**Outside the comparison:** the value of the home itself, and the tax
treatment of the sale. The first is an asset, not a cost, and the second
depends on circumstances the page cannot see.

Points sit awkwardly. They are a fee paid now in exchange for a lower rate
later, and they belong in a total-cost comparison because the fee has to be
recovered through lower payments. The tools on this site do not model them,
so a comparison involving points needs that part done separately.

## The comparison you can actually run

Put both offers through the calculator with the same property tax, insurance
and HOA inputs, and read three numbers: the monthly payment, the total
interest and the payoff date. Then run the same loan with a larger down
payment and see whether the total interest moves by more than the money
released would earn elsewhere. That is a comparison rather than a feeling,
and it is a comparison the arithmetic can settle.
